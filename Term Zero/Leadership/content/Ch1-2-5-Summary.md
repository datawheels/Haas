## Chapter 1 — "Discovering Your Authentic Leadership"
**Bill George, Peter Sims, Andrew N. McLean, Diana Mayer — HBR, February 2007**

### Core argument
After 50+ years and 1,000+ studies, no one has found a universal set of traits that define an "ideal leader" — and that's a good thing. Trying to copy a leadership style just produces a persona people see through. The authors interviewed 125 leaders (ages 23–93, half CEOs, diverse backgrounds) — the largest leadership-development study of its kind — and found leadership doesn't come from traits. **It emerges from how people frame and make meaning of their own life story.**

### Key sections and ideas

**1. Learning from your life story**
- "The story of your life is not your life. It is your story." (John Barth) — what matters is your narrative, not the raw facts.
- Most authentic leaders trace their motivation to a *difficult* experience — job loss, illness, death of someone close, exclusion, rejection — not just positive mentors.
- The key move: they didn't see themselves as victims. They **reframed** hardship into meaning and used it to find their purpose.
- Extended case: **Daniel Vasella** (Novartis CEO) — chronic childhood illness, family deaths, an absent mother, a doctor's small act of compassion during a painful medical procedure — all of it later became the direct source of the "compassion, competence, competition" culture he built at Novartis.

**2. Knowing your authentic self — self-awareness**
- Stanford GSB's Advisory Council named self-awareness the single most important leadership capability.
- **Denial** is the biggest obstacle. Case: David Pottruck (former Charles Schwab CEO) — was blindsided by feedback that colleagues didn't trust him despite his hard work; it took a second divorce and a counselor's blunt line ("you have a husband-behavior problem") before he really absorbed it.
- Self-awareness requires being willing to hear the feedback you don't want to hear.

**3. Practicing your values and principles**
- You don't actually know your values until they're tested under pressure — it's easy to "live your values" when things are going well.
- Leadership principles = values translated into action.
- Case: **Jon Huntsman** (Huntsman Corp) refused an ethically dubious order from H.R. Haldeman (Nixon's chief of staff) to entrap a congressman — and resigned rather than comply. "My inner moral compass made itself noticed."

**4. Balancing extrinsic and intrinsic motivation**
- Extrinsic = external validation (money, status, promotions). Intrinsic = personal meaning (growth, helping others, causes).
- Warning from Debra Dunn (HP): the path to material success is clearly marked and easy to default into; avoiding that requires actively knowing what actually fulfills you.
- Quote worth keeping: "If you're just chasing the rabbit around the course, you're not running toward anything meaningful." (Alice Woodwark, McKinsey)

**5. Building your support team**
- No leader succeeds alone. Authentic leaders build a deliberate support structure — spouse/partner, family, mentors, close friends, colleagues — built over time through shared history and openness.
- You need at least one person with whom you can be completely yourself and still be accepted.
- Examples of concrete structures: Bill George's own men's group, meeting weekly since 1974; Tad Piper's AA group as a support structure built on radical honesty.

**6. Integrating your life — staying grounded**
- Metaphor: think of your life as a house with rooms for personal life, work, family, friends — can you knock down the walls and be the same person in every room?
- Integration takes discipline, especially under stress, when it's easiest to become reactive.
- John Donahoe (eBay): "There is no nirvana... the trade-offs don't get any easier as you get older," but an integrated life makes you a *more* effective leader, not less.

**7. Empowering people to lead**
- The mark of authentic leadership isn't personal success — it's building empowered leaders at every level of the organization.
- Case: **Anne Mulcahy** (Xerox) — took over a company with $18B in debt and an SEC investigation, with no finance/manufacturing background. Her leverage was 25 years of built relationships and personal credibility. She met the top 100 executives personally to ask if they'd stay; paid back $10B in debt; tripled the stock price.

### The 8 self-reflection questions the article ends on
1. Which people/experiences in your early life had the greatest impact on you?
2. What tools do you use to become self-aware? What are the moments you say "this is the real me"?
3. What are your most deeply held values, and where did they come from?
4. What motivates you extrinsically vs. intrinsically — how do you balance them?
5. What does your support team look like?
6. Is your life integrated — are you the same person across every part of it?
7. What does being authentic mean in your life? Have you paid a price for it?
8. What can you do today, tomorrow, and this year to develop your authentic leadership?

## Chapter 2 — "What Does It Take to Build a Culture of Belonging?"
**Julia Taylor Kennedy and Pooja Jain-Link — HBR.org, June 2021 (Coqual research)**

### Core argument
In the wake of Covid-19 and 2020's racial-justice reckoning, DE&I work risks becoming divisive ("what about me?"). The more universal, unifying lever is **belonging** — a need psychologists rank on par with the need for love. Belonging is the mechanism that lets a company pursue equity work without alienating people who feel left out of the conversation.

### Coqual's quantifiable definition of belonging
You belong at work when you are:
1. **Seen** — for your unique contributions
2. **Connected** — to your coworkers
3. **Supported** — in daily work and career development
4. **Proud** — of your organization's values and purpose

### Key findings (from a nationally representative survey + focus groups, Feb & May 2020)
- High belonging scores correlate strongly with engagement, loyalty, and willingness to promote the company as a good place to work.
- Belonging scores vary significantly by group: **white men and white women score highest; Asian and Black women score lowest.**
- Men in the majority group (generally white men) who actively believe in DE&I have *higher* belonging scores than peers who don't — engaging with inclusion work builds your own sense of belonging, not just others'.

### Concrete actions, by role
- **Organization-wide:** spotlight role models; "representation matters" — diversify leadership and make it visible.
- **Senior leaders:** be inclusive — model it by sharing personal stories, listening to all voices (including those who feel "othered"), being transparent, creating access points like open office-hour Zoom slots for any employee.
- **Managers:** be responsive, appreciative, empowering — carry the culture day to day. Example: a quick feedback email to each team member at the close of a project.
- **Colleagues:** be respectful and supportive, and actually give feedback — respecting people's nonwork commitments and offering open, substantive feedback both build belonging.

### Why it matters for this course
This is the most directly "data + framework" of the three readings — useful as a model for how to structure a persuasive piece that opens with a real tension (DE&I fatigue/divisiveness), reframes it around a more universal need (belonging), backs it with a quantified model (4-part definition, survey data), and ends in concrete, role-specific calls to action.

## Chapter 5 — "Storytelling That Moves People: A Conversation with Screenwriting Coach Robert McKee"
**HBR interview by Bronwyn Fryer, June 2003**

### Core argument
Executives default to rhetoric — PowerPoint, statistics, authorities cited — because that's what they're trained in. McKee's claim: **rhetoric only persuades intellectually, and people are not moved to act by reason alone.** Story is the more powerful tool because it fuses an idea with emotion.

### Why rhetoric fails
1. Your audience has their own competing set of authorities and statistics — while you argue, they're mentally arguing back.
2. Even if you win the argument, you've only won it intellectually — that's not enough to move someone to act.

### What a story actually is
- A story shows **how and why life changes.** It starts in a state of relative balance, then an "inciting incident" throws it out of balance, and the protagonist's expectations collide with an uncooperative reality.
- The core engine of story: the gap between what someone *wants* and the forces (internal or external) working against it.
- Example walkthrough: a biotech CEO pitching investors. The flat version — market size, business plan, org chart — gets polite nods and stifled yawns. The story version — starts with his father's fatal heart attack, follows the science through an FDA rejection, a co-founder defecting, a funding crunch, and a patent race, resolving in approval and an IPO — gets bankers "throwing money at him."

### Why embracing the "dark side" makes a story more persuasive, not less
- Positive-only narratives read as spin and breed distrust — audiences already know things are never that easy.
- McKee's key distinction: **fear** is not knowing what will happen; **dread** is knowing and being unable to stop it. Great stories confront dread honestly rather than repressing it.
- A storyteller should position the real struggle in the foreground, not sweep it under the rug — it's what makes the audience trust you and see you as dynamic rather than a marketing department.

### How to find the story in anything (McKee's method)
Ask, in order:
1. What does my protagonist want, to restore balance? (Desire — "the blood of a story," not a shopping list.)
2. What's stopping them — internal doubt/fear, personal conflict, social forces, physical/natural obstacles, time pressure?
3. How would the protagonist act to get what they want, in the face of those obstacles? (Character is revealed by choices made under pressure.)
4. Step back and ask: "Do I believe this? Is it honest, even if the truth is unflattering?"

### On leadership and storytelling
- Good storytelling isn't just a communication skill — it requires and reflects self-knowledge. "A storyteller creates all characters from the self by asking: if I were this character, what would I do?"
- McKee links great leaders to great storytellers via a shared trait: skepticism balanced with self-insight and compassion — they see behind the "masks" people (including themselves) wear, without becoming cynical.
- Closing example: a factory worker, injured on the line and wrongly suspected of insurance fraud, who still wanted nothing more than to get back to work — an unused story that, if told by the company's CEO, could have inspired "redoubled effort from all the employees who heard it."

### Why it matters for this course
This chapter is the most directly load-bearing for your presentation assignment — it's essentially the theoretical backbone for `Ask.md`'s instruction to lead with story and let "data support the emotional case." McKee's four-question method (desire → obstacle → action under pressure → honesty check) is a usable diagnostic for pressure-testing your own draft's personal story.

## Cross-chapter threads worth noting for class discussion
- **Ch. 1 and Ch. 5 both argue meaning comes from confronting difficulty, not avoiding it** — George's leaders reframe hardship into purpose; McKee's storytellers position struggle in the foreground rather than a "rosy picture."
- **Ch. 1's "authentic self" and Ch. 5's "self-knowledge is the root of all great storytelling"** are close to the same claim from two different disciplines (leadership research vs. screenwriting) — both treat honest self-examination as the prerequisite for persuading or leading others.
- **Ch. 2 is the odd one out structurally** — it's the most data/framework-driven (a quantified 4-part model, survey scores by demographic group) and the most explicitly about a systemic/organizational lever rather than individual narrative — useful contrast if the discussion is about *when* to lead with story vs. when to lead with a model + data.
