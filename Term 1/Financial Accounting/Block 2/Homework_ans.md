
Problem 1 – Revenue recognition 
Amazon’s total revenue includes revenue from a few segments – each with its unique characteristics. Details about the revenue recognition policies are disclosed in the note Discussion of Business, Accounting policies, and Supplemental Disclosures. The discussion about revenue recognition is in the paragraphs after the title Revenue. 
1 – Summarize how Amazon records revenue in the following segments:

- Retail Sales:
 Consists of sales online and physical stores. Revenue is recognized when control of the goods is transferred to the customer, which generally occurs upon our delivery to a third-party carrier or, in the case of an Amazon delivery, to the customer. Amazon is primary here and recognizes full price as revenue

- Third-party sellers 
 Amazon is not seller but enable 3rd part sellers to sell their products at Amazon stores, and fulfill orders using amazon services. Revenue consists of commissions and any related fulfillment and shipping fees, which occurs upon delivery of the related products to a third-party carrier or to the customer

- AWS 
Revenue is recognized over time as services(compute, storage, database, and other services) — typically monthly, based on usage, since customers are billed per usage. Multi-year committed contracts get revenue recognized ratably/based on usage over the contract term, not upfront.

1 – Prior to 2024, Amazon used five years as the estimated useful life of servers and networking equipment, and it depreciates physical assets under the straight-line method. At the beginning of 2024 it extended the useful life to six years. Then, effective January 2025, Amazon reversed course and shortened the life back to five years for a subset of this equipment, citing the increased pace of technology development. Amazon forecasts approximately $220B of capital expenditures for 2026, largely in servers and networking equipment to support its AI business. Assume these investments are completed at the beginning of 2026.
What is the difference in depreciation expenses under the assumptions for five and for six years of useful life? What is the difference in pre-tax income under these two assumptions? Is the difference material? 
If you are the finance lead recommending the change, what rationale would you use to defend the change? If you are the investor, how would you challenge the change? 

Depreciating the full $220B of 2026 capex over a 5-year life produces annual depreciation expense of $44B, 
versus $36.7B under a 6-year life 
— a difference of ~$7.3B per year. 
Since depreciation is the only variable changing between the two scenarios, this flows dollar-for-dollar into pre-tax income: pre-tax income would be ~$7.3B lower under the 5-year assumption than under the 6-year assumption.
This difference is material. Measured against 2025 operating income ($80.0B) and pre-tax income ($97.3B), it represents roughly 9.1% and 7.5% respectively. 
2025's actual operating margin expanded from 
(2024) : 79,975/716,924  ~ 10.75% 
2025:  9- / 717 ~ 11.15% 
but absorbing the extra $7.3B of depreciation would turn that into operating margin contraction, down to ~10.1%. 
A swing that flips "margins are expanding" into "margins are contracting" — not just a modest change at the edges — is precisely the kind of effect that crosses from immaterial to material, even before applying any formal quantitative threshold.

As Finacial lead
- Pace of AI chip anf hardware innovation has accelerated sharply - equipment is no longer competitive in the market beyong 5 years in the market. 
- Runing older harware longer carries real cost like high pwer consumption and lower performance per dollar relative to newer chips - so upgrading keeps amazon's AI infrastructure competitive, for high more expense sooner

As investor
- I'd be skeptical of the pattern. The useful life has now moved twice in two years (5→6→5), and this latest shortening happens to take effect right as $220B of new capex is about to enter service.
- I'd also push back on the lack of independent evidence: what specifically changed about observed obsolescence rates, trade-in/resale values, or decommissioning ages that justifies this revision, rather than taking management's "increased pace of technology development" at face value? 
- And I'd note the longer-term effect: front-loading depreciation now means less depreciation drag in later years once this equipment is fully written off sooner — which conveniently sets up easier earnings comparisons down the road, a dynamic worth flagging even if it doesn't prove intent.
