## 1. Coffee Life — the Q1 2020 cash flow statement

**The story:** even though Coffee Life is privately held, its executive team holds quarterly investor meetings that function like public-company earnings calls, and **the cash flow statement always draws the most attention**. Erica has the BS and IS for Q1 2020 ready and gathers four additional facts to build the CF statement and prepare her talking points:
- The old roasting machine (see Class 5) was sold for **$1.2M**, generating a **$200K gain** reported inside "Interest income and other" on the income statement.
- Total cash spent on CapEx, including the new zero-emissions roaster, was **$3.5M**.
- Coffee Life **repaid $1M of long-term debt** (no new long-term borrowing), and **$1.2M of short-term debt** came due and was repaid, mostly refinanced with a new short-term loan.
- Coffee Life **paid $350K in dividends** during the quarter.

She anticipates three investor questions: (1) how did Coffee Life fund $3.5M of CapEx, debt repayment, and a dividend while barely moving its cash balance? (2) what drove operating cash flow this quarter, and is it sustainable? (3) what should next quarter's cash flow statement look like?

### The Q1 2020 income statement (for context)
```
Net sales                        6,553
Cost of sales                   (2,883)
Gross profit                     3,670
Total operating expenses        (2,709)
Operating income                   961    (14.7% operating margin)
Interest expense                  (190)
Interest income and other          207    (includes the $200K gain on the machine sale)
Income before income taxes         978
Provision for income taxes        (201)
Net income                         777
EPS                               0.6475
```

### The completed Q1 2020 cash flow statement, in full

**Cash flow from operations:**
```
Net income                                    777    (= Q1 2020 IS)
+ Depreciation and amortization               520
+ Other non-cash adjustments                (200)    ← backs OUT the $200K gain on the machine sale:
                                                          it's an investing-related item embedded in
                                                          net income, not an operating cash flow
Δ Accounts receivable                       1,529     (AR fell 3,579 → 2,050: a cash inflow)
Δ Inventories                                 564     (inventory fell 2,114 → 1,550: a cash inflow)
Δ Accounts payable                            255     (AP rose 1,315 → 1,570: a cash inflow)
Δ Other operating assets/liabilities          304
Net cash flow from operations (CFO)         3,749
```

**Cash flow from investing:**
```
Capital expenditures                       (3,500)    ← matches the "$3.5M CapEx" data point exactly
Sale of property (the old roaster)          1,200     ← matches the "$1.2M sale" data point exactly
Net cash flow from investing (CFI)         (2,300)
```

**Cash flow from financing:**
```
Proceeds from ST borrowings                 1,117
Repayments of ST borrowings                (1,200)    ← matches the "$1.2M ST loan came due" data point
Proceeds from LT borrowings                     0      ← matches "no new long-term borrowings"
Repayments of LT borrowings                (1,000)    ← matches the "$1M of LT debt" repayment exactly
Cash dividends paid                          (350)    ← matches the "$350K dividends" data point exactly
Net cash flow from financing (CFF)         (1,433)
```

**Reconciliation:**
```
Net change in cash (CFO + CFI + CFF) = 3,749 − 2,300 − 1,433 = 16
Beginning cash (= Q4 2019 ending balance)                     = 500
Ending cash                                                    = 516   ← ties exactly to the Q1 2020 balance sheet
```

### Answering Erica's anticipated investor questions

**Q1 — "How did we spend $3.5M on new equipment, repay debt, and pay a dividend, and still barely move the cash balance?"**
Because **operating cash flow was unusually strong** — $3,749K, nearly five times net income of $777K — not because of the $1.2M sale proceeds, which were a comparatively small piece of the puzzle. That $3,749K of CFO, not the equipment sale, is what actually funded the $3.5M of CapEx, the $1M LT debt repayment, and the $350K dividend, while cash only moved by a net $16K.

**Q2 — "What drove CFO this quarter, and is it sustainable?"**
The strength came almost entirely from a large, favorable working-capital swing: **AR fell $1,529K and inventory fell $564K in a single quarter** — both unusually large one-time releases of cash relative to a $777K net income base. This pattern is worth being cautious about, not celebrating: falling receivables and inventory in Q1 2020 line up with the COVID-19 demand shock referenced elsewhere in the Coffee Life materials (see the Class 3 AR case) — it's more consistent with slowing sales and customers stretching to keep current than with a genuine efficiency improvement. A company can't keep shrinking AR and inventory quarter after quarter without eventually shrinking the underlying business, so this level of CFO should **not** be read as a sign of a strengthening operating trend.

**Q3 — "What should next quarter look like?"**
Expect CFO to **normalize downward** once the one-time AR/inventory releases stop repeating, and CFI to shrink sharply since the major equipment replacement (the roaster) is now behind Coffee Life. If underlying operating performance doesn't hold up on its own (without help from working-capital swings), the cash balance could actually start to decline in future quarters rather than stay roughly flat.

## 2. Cash flow statement practice exercise (separate from Coffee Life)

This is a standalone in-class drill using a different, unnamed company — deliberately simpler than Coffee Life because every adjustment is handed to you as a discrete, individually labeled fact, rather than something you have to infer from balance sheet changes across two Coffee Life files.

### Given data
```
Depreciation expense (current year)                        12,000
Prior-year accumulated depreciation                         25,000
Equipment purchased (cash)                                  31,000
Equipment sold: original cost 10,000, accum. dep. 7,000
   (net book value 3,000), sold for 4,000 cash → gain of    1,000
Dividend declared and paid during the year                   5,000
```

### Income statement (given)
```
Sales                        120,000
COGS                          (70,000)
Other operating expenses     (38,800)
Gain on sale of equipment       1,000
Net income                     12,200
```

### Balance sheet changes (prior year → current year)
| Account | Prior | Current | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 45,000 | 29,000 | (16,000) |
| Accounts receivable | 28,000 | 35,000 | +7,000 |
| Inventory | 38,000 | 41,000 | +3,000 |
| PP&E, net | 75,000 | 91,000 | +16,000 |
| Accounts payable | 27,000 | 36,000 | +9,000 |
| Wages payable | 1,400 | 1,200 | (200) |
| Note payable, long-term | 56,000 | 38,000 | (18,000) |
| Common stock and APIC | 76,600 | 88,600 | +12,000 |
| Retained earnings | 25,000 | 32,200 | +7,200 (= NI 12,200 − Dividends 5,000) |

### The completed cash flow statement (indirect method)

**Operations:**
```
Net income                              12,200
+ Depreciation                          12,000    (given directly)
− Gain on sale of equipment             (1,000)    (non-operating; the actual cash from the sale
                                                      shows up in investing instead)
Δ Accounts receivable                   (7,000)    (AR increased → cash use)
Δ Inventory                             (3,000)    (inventory increased → cash use)
Δ Accounts payable                       9,000     (AP increased → cash source)
Δ Wages payable                           (200)    (wages payable decreased → cash use)
Cash flow from operations               22,000
```

**Investing:**
```
Capital expenditures                   (31,000)    (given directly)
Proceeds from sale of equipment          4,000     (given directly)
Cash flow from investing               (27,000)
```

**Financing:**
```
Payment of dividends                    (5,000)    (given directly)
Equity issuance (Δ Common stock+APIC)   12,000
Note payable repayment (Δ LT note)     (18,000)
Cash flow from financing               (11,000)
```

**Reconciliation:**
```
Net change in cash = 22,000 − 27,000 − 11,000 = (16,000)
Beginning cash = 45,000  →  Ending cash = 29,000
```
This matches the balance sheet's own cash change (45,000 → 29,000 = −16,000) exactly — the built-in answer-key check in the unfilled template even pre-computes this figure so you know what you're solving toward.

A useful side note buried in the "additional data": prior accumulated depreciation of $25,000 + current-year depreciation of $12,000 − the $7,000 of accumulated depreciation removed with the sold equipment = $30,000 ending accumulated depreciation. That reconciliation isn't a cash flow statement line item itself, but it's exactly the kind of check that confirms the $31,000 CapEx and $4,000 sale proceeds you were given are internally consistent with the PP&E and depreciation figures — a habit worth carrying into any case (like Coffee Life's) where CapEx isn't handed to you directly and has to be derived.

## Why pair this drill with the Coffee Life case in the same class
The two exercises are deliberately complementary rather than redundant. Coffee Life is the **realistic, continuity-heavy** version: several of the CF adjustments (the gain on the roaster sale, the working-capital swings) require you to remember facts and figures introduced in earlier classes (Class 5's depreciation case, Class 3's AR case) and to interpret what the resulting numbers mean for the business, not just compute them. The standalone practice exercise strips all of that away — every input is an isolated, individually labeled bullet point with nothing to infer — so it functions as the "clean drill" for the mechanical, indirect-method construction itself (starting from net income, adding back non-cash items, backing out gains, adjusting for each working-capital change) before you're asked to apply that same mechanic inside a messier, narrative-driven, cross-referenced case. Doing the clean version first (or immediately alongside) makes it far easier to trust your own arithmetic when Coffee Life's numbers get more complicated.

## Cross-cutting themes
1. **The indirect-method cash flow statement is really just "un-doing" accrual accounting** — every adjustment either removes a non-cash item that inflated or deflated net income (depreciation, the gain on sale) or captures the cash effect of a balance-sheet change that net income doesn't reflect (AR, inventory, AP, wages payable).
2. **A gain or loss on the sale of an asset must be backed out of CFO and the actual cash proceeds reported in full under investing** — otherwise the gain gets silently double-counted (once through net income, once through the investing proceeds).
3. **Strong operating cash flow is not automatically good news** — Coffee Life's Q1 2020 CFO looked excellent on the surface, but the underlying driver (AR and inventory both shrinking sharply) is exactly the pattern you'd expect from a demand slowdown, not from genuine operating improvement. Reading *why* a number moved matters as much as the number itself.
4. **Financing and investing lines are frequently just given, not derived** — in the practice exercise, CapEx, sale proceeds, and dividends are handed to you directly, whereas Coffee Life sometimes requires deriving CapEx-like figures from balance sheet changes (as seen in Class 2's original FY2019 statement). Knowing when a number can be pulled straight from the data versus when it has to be backed into is itself a skill this pairing is designed to build.
