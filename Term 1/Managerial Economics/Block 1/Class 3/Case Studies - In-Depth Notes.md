## 1. "He Earns $33 an Hour as a Costco Cashier. Now He's a Millionaire." (WSJ, July 8, 2026)

**The facts:** Tony Barzar, a 60-year-old Costco cashier of ~40 years in Tucson, AZ, earns **$32.90/hr** (Costco recently raised the top hourly rate from $31.90 to $32.90) and has **over $1 million** in his 401(k). Costco's turnover after one year is **~7%**, "a fraction of industry averages." McKinsey's 2023 study of 100+ retailers found top-quartile employee-satisfaction firms are more than twice as likely to also be top-quartile in customer satisfaction, and estimated **losing a front-line retail employee costs an average of $10,000**. Tucson general manager Travis Maze: "When a long-term employee leaves, the average wage paid to his 380 Tucson employees goes down, which helps profits, 'but it comes at the expense of experience.'" Costco's stock is up over 2,000% since 2008.

### How this relates to opportunity cost (class.md's framing question)
Standard hourly-wage accounting treats Barzar's $32.90/hr as simply an *expense* — a direct line item, higher than a replacement worker's starting wage would be. But the **opportunity cost of replacing him** (or any long-tenured worker) is much larger than the wage differential alone, exactly the "Result 11 — cost causation" logic from Theory Notes: the *true* cost of high turnover includes (a) McKinsey's estimated **$10,000-per-departure** direct replacement/training cost, (b) the lost mentoring value long-tenured "culture coach" employees provide to newer hires (explicitly why Costco created that role), and (c) the McKinsey correlation between employee satisfaction and customer satisfaction, implying lost sales/reputation that never shows up as a discrete line item. Manager Maze's own quote is the clearest statement of the point: a naive accountant looking only at average wage paid would see turnover as *profit-improving* ("the average wage paid... goes down, which helps profits") — precisely the **Average Cost Fallacy** from Theory Notes §4: the "on average" wage bill looks better after a senior worker leaves, but the *marginal* cost of losing accumulated experience and culture (much harder to price, but real) is what the McKinsey $10,000 figure and the "expense of experience" comment are trying to capture. Costco's higher-than-market wages and extended benefits (co-pays of $15/$25, extra vacation after 30 years) are best understood as an **investment against a real, if not directly booked, opportunity cost** — the same logic Class 2's opportunity-cost chapter applies to Pasona's "free" labor and space: treating retention/turnover as a pure wage-expense question, ignoring the opportunity cost of lost experience and culture, would lead a naive accountant toward the wrong staffing decision, just as it did with Fenmark's managers (Class 1) and the "shared overhead" tutoring example (Theory Notes §2).

## 2. "Chinese Solar Farms Are Crowding Out Much-Needed Crops" (WSJ, Chun Han Wong, Aug. 1, 2024)

**The facts:** China installed more solar capacity in one year (2023) than the U.S. has built in its entire history — grid-connected solar capacity grew from near-zero in 2012 to nearly **700+ gigawatts by mid-2024**, up 55% in 2023 alone. Analysts estimate China needs to expand solar capacity **~14×** from 2020 levels to hit Xi Jinping's 2060 carbon-neutrality target. But Xi has also drawn an "arable land red line" of ~300 million acres, and China lost **18+ million acres** of arable land in the decade to 2019. A 2018 study estimated global food-production losses from solar-on-cropland reached enough to feed **4.3 million people for a year**, a third attributable to China. In one Hubei township, farmland yields fell **~80%** after a solar developer's superficial "agrivoltaic" (dual-use) effort. Analyst Cosimo Ries: "There is a lot of money to be made from renewables, while agriculture is a low-value and low-profit business."

### How this relates to opportunity cost (class.md's framing question)
This is opportunity cost playing out at **national-policy scale** rather than firm scale. Land used for a solar farm has a real market opportunity cost equal to its best alternative use — here, food production. Ries's quote is the market-price signal driving the problem: solar (especially subsidized) generates far more revenue per acre than low-margin agriculture, so **local officials and farmers rationally convert farmland to solar even when the true social opportunity cost (lost food security) is much larger than the private market signal captures** — a classic externality, structurally identical to the Pasona case's "free" real estate (Class 2 notes): just as Pasona's ¥20M cost estimate ignored the opportunity cost of prime downtown real estate, China's solar-farm economics (as experienced by individual developers and local governments) ignore the opportunity cost of food security that only shows up in aggregate, nationally, later. Beijing's regulatory response — banning solar development on protected farmland, fining violators, promoting "agrivoltaic" dual-use, mandating regional reviews — is best understood as **forcing private decision-makers to internalize an opportunity cost that market prices alone weren't reflecting**, the same conceptual move as Amazon's IPI policy below.

## 3. "Amazon's new warehouse policy will penalize sellers for storing products too long" (CNBC, Eugene Kim, June 29, 2018)

**The facts:** Amazon's warehouse footprint exceeded 100 million sq. ft., growing ~30–35%/year; industrial real-estate demand had outstripped supply for **32 consecutive quarters**. In 2017, Amazon's fulfillment costs (its biggest operating expense) surged **43% to $25.2 billion** while total sales grew only 31% — costs outpacing revenue. Starting July 1, 2018, Amazon introduced the **Inventory Performance Index (IPI)**, a 0–1,000 score measuring how well a seller manages inventory (removing unsold products, fixing listings, avoiding excess dwell time). Sellers scoring below **350** face storage restrictions and monthly "overage fees"; those above 350 face no restrictions. Previously, "sellers could rent unlimited storage regardless of how effectively they managed their inventory" (Chad Rubin, CEO of Skubana).

### Q1 — Why was there a need to create the new policy?
Before the IPI, warehouse storage was effectively a **shared overhead cost with no causal link back to the sellers who caused it** — exactly the Theory Notes §2 "Shared Overhead Fallacy" setup, but running in Amazon's favor by default (unlimited "free" storage meant sellers had zero marginal incentive to move slow inventory, since they bore none of the true cost of the space they occupied). The result was congestion Amazon "can't open fulfillment centers quickly enough" to keep pace with — a real capacity/cost crunch (fulfillment costs +43% vs. sales +31%) with no internal price signal forcing sellers to economize on the warehouse space they were consuming.

### Q2 — How does the new IPI score account for the costs third-party sellers impose on Amazon's warehouse?
The IPI is a direct, real-world application of **Result 11, cost causation** (Theory Notes §7/§2): *"if it can't be causally allocated, don't allocate it at all — but if you can causally allocate it, you should."* Rather than spreading warehouse costs evenly (or not at all) across all sellers regardless of behavior, the IPI **ties a seller's own storage limits and fees directly to that seller's own inventory-dwell behavior** — a seller who moves inventory quickly (low dwell time, few stale listings) gets an IPI near 1,000 and unrestricted storage; a seller who lets inventory sit (the actual behavior causing the congestion Amazon is fighting) gets penalized with overage fees once their score drops below 350. This converts a previously invisible, un-allocated "shared" warehouse cost into a **marginal cost sellers directly face at their own decision margin** (keep slow-moving inventory in Amazon's warehouse vs. remove/discount it) — the same conceptual move as the "causal, not revenue-proportional" fix to the tutoring overhead example. The one flagged imperfection (the seasonal-seller problem, e.g., gift-basket seller Ephraim Ausch, whose IPI would otherwise cratered every off-season) is itself informative: a purely mechanical causal allocation can still misfire if the "cause" (inventory dwell time) is itself seasonal/predictable rather than a sign of true inefficiency — Amazon's ad hoc custom accommodations for seasonal sellers show the policy is still being tuned.

## 4. "Thanks to Nvidia, AI Will Soon Take Your Order at Taco Bell and Pizza Hut" (WSJ, Isabelle Bousquette, March 18, 2025)

**The facts:** Yum Brands (Taco Bell, Pizza Hut, KFC, Habit Burger & Grill; 61,000+ restaurants) partnered with Nvidia to build **AI-powered voice ordering** for drive-thru and phone, rolling out to 500 restaurants starting Q2 2025, built in-house by Yum's ~2,000 technologists using Nvidia's tools/GPUs (rather than buying prepackaged vendor apps — "we want to own the intellectual property," says CDTO Joe Park). Digital-channel orders are already above 50% (up from 19% in 2019), and Park says digital customers spend more (upsell, personalization, notifications). In-restaurant workers aren't eliminated — they shift toward customer service/hospitality.

### Q1 — What issues determine the costs and benefits of adopting AI for drive-thru orders?
**Costs:** a large **fixed** upfront investment — building proprietary AI voice-ordering software with Nvidia's tools/GPU compute, deploying 2,000 technologists, developing the "Byte by Yum" platform — incurred once regardless of how many restaurants ultimately use it (classic fixed cost, Theory Notes §3). **Benefits:** each additional restaurant that adopts the system costs comparatively little in **marginal** terms (software, once built, redeploys cheaply across the chain's 61,000 locations) — combined with the demonstrated digital-channel revenue lift (upsell/personalization) and potential order-accuracy gains from computer vision, this is a textbook large-fixed-cost/low-marginal-cost technology investment, whose average cost per restaurant keeps falling the more locations adopt it (the mirror image of the combs example's declining-then-rising AC curve, Theory Notes §4 — except here Yum is trying to stay purely on the *falling* part of the curve by scaling to as many restaurants as possible).

### Q2 — How would waiting for the technology to improve impact these costs and benefits?
This is a **real-options** question (Class 2 Theory Notes §3, and Class 1 Theory Notes §6): waiting lets Yum benefit from further Nvidia GPU/software cost declines and better AI accuracy, at the cost of delaying the demonstrated digital-revenue uplift and ceding ground to competitors (the article notes McDonald's is running similar digital experiments, "with mixed" results). Two factors specifically favor *not* waiting, by the logic of Theory Notes §3's lemonade-stand example: (1) **reversibility is high** — because Yum built the system in-house and owns the IP/platform rather than locking into a vendor contract, it can iteratively update the software as the technology improves, rather than making one irreversible bet on a fixed vendor product; this is the same "downside is bounded, so upside required is smaller" logic that lowered the lemonade-stand threshold from $100 to $83.33. (2) The 500-restaurant initial rollout (vs. deploying to all 61,000 at once) is itself a real option: a **staged/delay-like structure** that lets Yum learn from a limited real-world test before committing further capital — directly parallel to Hermalin's "delay" example (Class 1 Theory Notes §6), trading a bounded cost of delay against the option value of better information before full-scale commitment.

### Q3 — How does this adoption change fixed versus variable costs?
It **shifts cost structure away from variable, per-labor-hour costs (human order-takers, paid per shift, scaling roughly linearly with hours of operation) toward a large fixed cost (the AI platform, built once) with near-zero marginal cost per additional order or additional restaurant** once deployed — the mirror image of the combs example's cost structure, but applied to labor substitution rather than a single product line. Crucially, per Park, in-restaurant human labor doesn't disappear — it's redeployed to hospitality/customer-service functions — so some **variable** labor cost persists, but its *function* changes from "order-taking" (now largely fixed-cost AI) to "service recovery and personal touch" (still variable, scaling with foot traffic). This mirrors Theory Notes §4's core lesson: a firm facing a kinked or two-tier cost structure (like the combs' $0.80 vs. $1.05 marginal cost regimes) should expect its optimal input mix, not just its optimal quantity, to shift once the relative marginal costs of automation vs. human labor cross over — exactly what's happening industry-wide as AI voice-ordering marginal costs keep falling toward zero.

## 5. Brand X — brief preview (fully assigned at the start of Block 2, not solved here)

`Brand_X.pdf` + `Brand_X_Data.xls` present a young Brand X executive puzzled by why the price of the firm's proprietary Chemical Z (no direct competitors, patent/trade-secret protected) swings quarter to quarter — e.g., $3.06/gallon this quarter, selling 4.2M gallons, vs. $3.07/gallon three years earlier for essentially the same 4.2M gallons — even though demand looks stable. Brand X has always used a **cost-plus pricing rule** (unit cost + a fixed 20% markup), and the Quarter-40 cost breakdown shows raw materials (a market-priced input locked in at the start of each quarter, currently $1.69/gallon of a $2.55 total unit cost) as by far the largest and most volatile cost component — while labor and depreciation are comparatively stable. `Brand_X_Data.xls` provides 40 quarters of Profit, Demand, Price, and raw-material-share-of-unit-cost. The assignment (three questions: judge the pricing strategy; recommend an alternative; identify concerns with your recommendation) is exactly a large-scale, real-data test of this class's central lesson — **whether cost-plus/average-cost pricing is the right rule when demand looks essentially price-inelastic (stable quantity despite price swings) and marginal cost, not average cost, should be driving the price/quantity decision** (Theory Notes §4–5). This is picked up in full at the start of Block 2 and not solved in these notes.

## Cross-cutting themes
1. **"Free" or "shared" costs are a recurring trap, and the fix is always the same: causally allocate, or don't allocate at all.** The Shared Overhead Fallacy (Theory Notes §2), Amazon's IPI, and China's solar-farmland conflict are the same structural problem — cross-referencing directly against Class 2's opportunity-cost machinery (Pasona's "free" real estate, the "free" cloud services) and Class 1's Fenmark case (comparing restaurants without accounting for structurally different demand).
2. **Average cost tells you whether to operate; marginal cost tells you how much.** This is the single idea every article in this class illustrates from a different angle: Costco's average-wage-after-turnover framing hides the true marginal cost of lost experience; Amazon's old flat storage policy ignored the marginal cost individual sellers impose; Yum's AI investment only pays off once its near-zero marginal cost is spread across enough restaurants to outweigh the large fixed development cost.
3. **Real options (reversibility, staged rollouts, "wait and see") reduce the cost of uncertainty**, whether the decision is a corporate one (Yum's 500-restaurant pilot, building in-house for future flexibility) or a broader systems one (China's shift toward reversible "agrivoltaic" dual-use policy rather than an outright, irreversible ban) — tying directly back to Class 1 and Class 2's decision-tree and real-options theory.
