## 1. WSJ — "Why Empty Seats at Taylor Swift's Concerts Are Good for Business"

**The setup.** Concert venues routinely have visibly empty seats even for the hottest tours (Taylor Swift's Eras Tour among them), while resale/scalper prices for the same shows run many multiples of face value. The article frames this as a puzzle: why would a profit-maximizing promoter leave both seats *and* obvious revenue on the table?

**Discussion question 1 — Is it possible that profit-maximizing prices can result in empty seats?**

Yes, and the theory notes explain exactly why. Under **simple (uniform) pricing**, the promoter picks a single face-value price $p^*$ using the MR=MC rule. Because a stadium's marginal cost per additional attendee is close to zero (a fixed cost of staging the show, small marginal costs of servicing one more fan), the *profit-maximizing* $p^*$ under simple pricing is **not** the market-clearing price — it is instead set where $MR(q^*)=MC(q^*)\approx 0$, i.e., roughly where demand crosses the **unitary-elastic point** ($\varepsilon_D=-1$; Result 28). If face value $p^*$ is set below the true market-clearing price (which is common when venues intentionally underprice relative to what secondary markets reveal — whether for goodwill, fairness, or long-run brand reasons), then at $p^*$ **quantity demanded can exceed capacity**, and the promoter would, in principle, want to sell more seats than exist. Conversely, empty seats at a *high* face-value price are consistent with simple pricing too: if $p^*$ sits above the market-clearing price for the *marginal*, price-sensitive buyer, some low-valuation seats go unsold even though $MR=MC$ has been satisfied for the seats the promoter actually wanted to sell. In both directions, **the profit-maximizing single price under simple pricing generally does not equal the market-clearing price**, precisely because simple pricing (Theory Notes §3) forces one uniform price across a heterogeneous population of fans with very different willingness to pay — exactly the "money left on the table" problem that Lecture Note 5's Advanced Pricing material (Class 5) exists to solve. Put simply: empty seats are the observable *symptom* of a monopolist using a blunt, single-price instrument on a demand curve that isn't uniform.

There is a second, complementary explanation: some seats may be deliberately withheld from the face-value pool (held for VIP packages, sponsors, or resale via the venue's own channels) — a form of price discrimination/market segmentation that intentionally looks like "empty seats" in the ordinary sales channel while the true allocation is optimized elsewhere.

**Discussion question 2 — What is the effect of Ticketmaster's Verified Fan program on the possibility to scalp tickets?**

Verified Fan tries to restrict ticket purchases to (ostensibly) real fans rather than bots/resellers, by requiring registration and using algorithms to filter out suspected scalpers before the sale. In the language of Theory Notes §3, **scalping is a form of arbitrage**: because face value $p^*$ is set below the true market-clearing price for many buyers (see above), a large gap opens up between face value and what the highest-willingness-to-pay fans would actually pay. Anyone who can buy at $p^*$ and resell at the higher market price captures that gap as pure arbitrage profit — with *no* value returned to the artist or promoter. Verified Fan's goal is to shrink the *pool of buyers who can resell* down toward the pool of buyers who actually want to attend, which should, if it worked perfectly, eliminate the arbitrage opportunity by making it harder for a bot/reseller to acquire tickets at $p^*$ in the first place. In practice, the program's imperfection (bots working around verification, fans still reselling if the resale price is attractive enough) means arbitrage is only *dampened*, not eliminated — consistent with the theory notes' general point that simple pricing "leaves money on the table" (Lecture Note 5 preview) whenever arbitrage can occur, and that the seller would always prefer a pricing scheme that captures that gap itself rather than ceding it to scalpers.

**Connection to theory:** This article is the clearest illustration in Class 4 of the gap between the *simple-pricing* profit-maximizing price (§7 of the Theory Notes) and what a monopolist could earn with a scheme that avoided the driving-down-the-price effect altogether — i.e., it previews the "Holy Grail of pricing" (perfect price discrimination) that Class 5's Theory Notes formalize.

## 2. WSJ — "Seeking Perfect Prices, CEO Tears Up the Rules" (Parker Hannifin)

**The setup.** Parker Hannifin, an industrial-parts manufacturer, historically priced using a flat **cost-plus markup rule** (marking every part up by roughly 35% over cost). New leadership scrapped this rule in favor of pricing that reflects what the market will actually bear for each specific part.

**Discussion question 1 — The article is clear on the pricing scheme Parker abandoned, but less clear on what replaced it. What factors now affect how Parker prices its parts? If you were selling a commodity product, would a standard markup rule be a good strategy?**

What replaced the flat rule, read through the Lerner markup rule (Theory Notes §8), is **price discrimination by elasticity**: Parker now prices each part according to how price-sensitive *that part's specific buyers* are, rather than applying one blanket percentage to its entire catalog. The economic factors that now drive price are exactly the inputs to $-1/\varepsilon_D$ for each part:
- **How replaceable/substitutable the part is** (does a competitor make an equivalent part? is it a proprietary or hard-to-source niche design?) — more substitutes means more elastic demand, which the Lerner rule says should command a *smaller* markup.
- **How critical the part is to the buyer's larger system** (a small, cheap part that's essential to keep an expensive machine running has very inelastic demand — the buyer will pay a large markup rather than risk downtime).
- **Buyer's own price sensitivity / how big a line item the part is in the buyer's budget** — a bigger share of the customer's spend usually means more elastic demand (the customer shops harder).
- **Competitive intensity and switching costs** specific to each niche.

For a **commodity product**, a flat/simple markup rule is actually much closer to correct: a commodity by definition has many close substitutes, meaning demand for any *one* seller's commodity output is close to perfectly elastic ($\varepsilon_D \to -\infty$). By the Lerner rule, $-1/\varepsilon_D \to 0$ — the economically "correct" markup for a true commodity converges toward a *small, roughly uniform* number across the whole commodity line, because every unit faces essentially the same (very high) elasticity. So yes: **a standard, low, roughly-uniform markup can be a perfectly sound strategy for a genuine commodity** — the problem at Parker was applying that commodity-style *uniform* logic to a portfolio that was actually a mix of commodity parts (elastic, should carry thin margins) and niche/proprietary parts (inelastic, should carry fat margins). A flat 35% rule systematically **overprices the commodity items** (losing volume/share to competitors who correctly charge less) and **underprices the niche items** (leaving Lerner-rule-implied profit on the table on parts customers would have paid much more for).

**Discussion question 2 — Firms and managers are often concerned about market share. How is the new pricing scheme likely to change the market shares of Parker's products?**

Following the elasticity logic above: on **commodity-like parts** (elastic demand, many substitutes), the new scheme should *lower* prices relative to the old flat 35% markup, because the Lerner-optimal markup for high $|\varepsilon_D|$ is smaller than 35%. Lower prices on those elastic items should **increase Parker's market share** in those product lines, since price-sensitive buyers are, by definition, the ones most responsive to a price cut. On **niche/proprietary parts** (inelastic demand, few or no substitutes), the new scheme should *raise* prices above the old flat markup — buyers of these parts are, by definition, the least likely to walk away over a price increase, so Parker should see **little to no share loss** on these lines even as prices (and margins) rise. Net effect: a **more polarized, elasticity-sorted market-share profile** — Parker becomes more price-competitive (and likely gains share) in commoditized categories while extracting more margin (with limited share risk) in specialized categories, rather than the old rule's "one-size-fits-all" markup that made Parker uncompetitive on commodities and left money on the table on specialties simultaneously.

**Connection to theory:** This is the single cleanest real-world application of the **Lerner markup rule** (Theory Notes §8, Result 30) and of the **cost-causation warnings from Lecture Note 3** (Theory Notes §2) — the case for abandoning a flat markup is fundamentally an elasticity argument, not a cost-accounting argument.

## 3. WSJ — "The Chocolate Company Where Prices Change Every Three Months"

**The setup.** A chocolate maker resets its prices on a fixed quarterly cadence rather than leaving them static for long stretches, explicitly tracking how input costs (cocoa, sugar, labor) and demand conditions shift, and adjusting markups accordingly — discussed in class specifically for its treatment of elasticity, markups, and the price-cost relationship.

**Discussion framing — elasticity, markups, and the relationship between price and costs.**

This article is the direct empirical illustration of **Example 15/16 in the Theory Notes** (§8): the Lerner rule doesn't just explain the *level* of an optimal markup, it explains how a firm should **respond to a cost shock**. Cocoa and sugar prices are volatile commodity inputs, so the chocolate company's marginal cost genuinely shifts quarter to quarter. Two distinct mechanisms are at work simultaneously, and the case is testing whether you can separate them:

1. **Cost pass-through (Example 16).** When marginal cost rises by some percentage $\delta$, the Lerner rule implies the profit-maximizing response is to raise price by *approximately the same percentage* $\delta$ — **holding elasticity fixed**. This is the mechanical, "pass the cost increase through to price" logic, and it's why a company facing real, ongoing input-cost volatility (unlike, say, a company with a stable cost base) has a genuine economic reason to **reprice frequently** rather than absorb cost swings into a fixed price for years at a time. Repricing quarterly (rather than continuously) is a practical compromise: it captures most of the benefit of tracking marginal cost without the customer-relations/menu cost of changing prices constantly.
2. **Markup as a function of elasticity, not just cost (Example 15).** The size of the price adjustment for *any given* cost shock also depends on $\varepsilon_D$ at the time: $p = MC/(1+1/\varepsilon_D)$ (rearranged from the Lerner rule). If elasticity itself is changing quarter to quarter (e.g., because of seasonal demand — chocolate demand around Valentine's Day/Easter/holidays is plausibly less price-elastic than in an off-season, since some purchases are less discretionary/more gift-driven), the "correct" repricing is **not** simply "cost went up X%, so price goes up X%" — it's a joint function of both the cost shock *and* the current elasticity. A firm that mechanically passes through cost changes without re-examining elasticity is leaving Lerner-rule-implied profit on the table exactly the way Parker Hannifin's flat 35% rule did.

**The price-cost relationship, explicitly connected to the markup formula:** the Theory Notes' Lerner rule, $(p^*-MC)/p^* = -1/\varepsilon_D$, is a statement that price and marginal cost should move together **but not one-for-one in every case** — the exact relationship is scaled by $-1/\varepsilon_D$. A chocolate company operating in a category with some genuine differentiation (brand, quality perception) but also real substitutes (other candy, other treats) sits somewhere in the elastic-but-not-perfectly-elastic middle ground — enough market power to sustain *some* markup above cost, but a low enough $|\varepsilon_D|$... (actually a large enough $|\varepsilon_D|$, i.e. meaningfully elastic demand) that markups stay modest and cost pass-through remains close to complete. This is the same theoretical apparatus as Parker Hannifin, applied to *time* (quarterly cost shocks) rather than to a *portfolio* (many different parts).

**Connection to theory:** Directly operationalizes Theory Notes §8, Examples 15–16 — the Lerner rule isn't just a static formula for setting price once; it's a *rule for repricing* whenever cost or demand conditions change, which is exactly the muscle a quarterly repricing cadence is built to exercise.

## Cross-cutting themes

1. **A single flat markup (or a single flat face price) is a simplification that only survives contact with reality when demand is genuinely homogeneous.** Taylor Swift's empty seats, Parker Hannifin's abandoned 35% rule, and even the chocolate company's willingness to reprice quarterly are all, underneath, stories about a firm discovering that **one price cannot be optimal across a heterogeneous population or across time**, and moving toward pricing that reflects elasticity more directly.
2. **The Lerner markup rule, $(p^*-MC)/p^* = -1/\varepsilon_D$, is the load-bearing formula for all three articles.** It explains why commodities should carry thin margins (Parker), why niche/proprietary items can carry fat margins (Parker), and why a firm facing real input-cost volatility should reprice on a cadence that tracks both cost *and* elasticity shifts (chocolate).
3. **Arbitrage is the enemy of any pricing scheme that isn't perfectly uniform.** Ticket scalping is arbitrage in its purest form: buying at an artificially low simple price and reselling at the market-clearing price. Every attempt to move beyond simple pricing (Verified Fan today; the whole of Class 5's price-discrimination toolkit) has to grapple with the same underlying constraint — arbitrage is only possible when simple pricing under-serves the true dispersion in buyer willingness to pay, which is precisely the condition all three of this class's articles describe.
4. **These articles are a bridge to Class 5.** Each one shows a firm *reaching the limits* of simple pricing (Theory Notes §3–8) and groping toward more sophisticated tools — differentiated pricing by segment (Parker), value-based ticket allocation (Taylor Swift), and cost/elasticity-tracking dynamic pricing (chocolate) — that Class 5's price-discrimination and two-part-tariff material formalizes.
