## 1. Where this fits in the course

Class 4 established that **simple (uniform) pricing** — one price, all units, all customers — necessarily leaves value on the table whenever buyers differ in willingness to pay. Class 5 formalizes exactly *how much* value is left on the table and works through the menu of pricing schemes a monopolist can use to capture more of it: **two-part tariffs, third-degree price discrimination (group pricing), and second-degree price discrimination (versioning/quantity discounts)**. This is the toolkit behind every "why do different people pay different prices for basically the same thing" article in this class.

## 2. What simple pricing loses (§5.1) — deadweight loss

Reconsider the profit-maximizing simple-pricing quantity $q^*$ (Class 4, Figure 4.8). If the firm could sell one *more* unit, the $q^*+1$st, **without** having to lower the price on the other $q^*$ units, it would profit by
```
P(q*+1) − MC(q*+1) > 0
```
since $MC(q^*+1) < P(q^*+1)$ (demand still exceeds marginal cost just past $q^*$). Under simple pricing, though, selling that extra unit requires lowering price on *all* units — the driving-down-the-price effect — which is exactly why the firm stops at $q^*$. Repeating this logic for every unit between $q^*$ and the point where $P(q)=MC(q)$ (call it $q^{**}$) shows the firm forgoes a triangular region of potentially profitable trades called the **deadweight loss** of simple pricing. **The entire motivation for every pricing scheme in this note is to recover some or all of this deadweight loss.**

## 3. Aggregate consumer surplus and the "Holy Grail" of pricing (§5.2–5.3)

**Result 31/32:** an individual's consumer surplus at price $p$ is the area under her demand curve from $p$ to infinity; **aggregate** consumer surplus $CS$ at price $p$ is the area under the *aggregate* demand curve $D(\cdot)$ from $p$ to infinity.

**Result 33:** the area under the inverse demand curve $P(\cdot)$ from 0 to $Q$ is **aggregate benefit**, $B(Q)$. **Result 34:** $P(q) = MB(q)$ — inverse demand *is* the marginal aggregate benefit schedule.

**Total welfare:** $W(q) = B(q) - C(q)$ — the entire economic pie generated by selling $q$ units. **A firm can never capture more profit than $W(q)$** (any more would mean consumers receive negative surplus, so they simply wouldn't buy).

**Result 35:** if a firm could capture *all* of $W(q)$, it would want to produce where inverse demand intersects marginal cost — i.e., $P(q^{**}) = MC(q^{**})$, the *efficient* quantity, with **zero deadweight loss**.

**This ideal outcome — capturing all of $W(q^{**})$ — is called perfect price discrimination, also known as first-degree price discrimination: the "Holy Grail" of pricing.** It is rarely achievable exactly, but every scheme below is best understood as an attempt to get *closer* to it than simple pricing does.

## 4. Two-part tariffs (§5.4)

**Definition:** a tariff with an **entry fee** $F$ (paid just to be allowed to buy at all — sunk once paid) plus a **per-unit charge** $p$. Total expenditure for $q>0$ units: $T(q) = pq + F$.

**Consumer's decision:** since the entry fee is sunk once paid, a consumer who pays it buys exactly as she would under simple pricing at price $p$: $q=d(p)$. She pays the fee only if $F \le cs(d(p))$ — the **participation constraint**.

### Homogeneous consumers — the Grail is achievable

**Result 36:** if all $N$ consumers have *identical* demand, the profit-maximizing two-part tariff sets:
(i) the per-unit charge $p$ equal to marginal cost at the efficient quantity, i.e., $P(q^{**})=MC(q^{**})$;
(ii) the entry fee $F$ equal to each consumer's entire consumer surplus, $cs(d(p))$, which equals $CS/N$.

**Result + derivation:** profit under this scheme reduces algebraically to $\pi = B(Q)-C(Q) = W(Q)$ — **a two-part tariff with homogeneous consumers achieves the Holy Grail (perfect price discrimination) exactly.** The firm sets price at marginal cost (efficient — no deadweight loss) and extracts *all* consumer surplus through the entry fee.

**Example 17 (amusement park, worked in full):** $MC=0$; 50,000 identical patrons, each with $d(p) = 50-12.5p$ for $p\le4$.
```
Per-unit charge: p = MC = 0   (ride tickets are "free" at the margin)

Entry fee = cs(d(0)):
  d(0) = 50, so the demand curve is a triangle w/ height 4, base 50
  Area = ½ × 4 × 50 = 100
  → Entry fee F = $100 per patron
```

**Example 18 (non-constant MC, worked in full):** $C(q)=q^2/2000$ (so $MC(q)=q/1000$); 1,000 identical customers, each with $d(p)=10-p$ for $p\le10$.
```
Aggregate demand: D(p) = 1000(10−p) = 10,000 − 1,000p  →  P(q) = 10 − q/1000

Set P(q) = MC(q):  10 − q/1000 = q/1000  ⟹  q** = 5000
Per-unit charge:  p = P(5000) = 5

Entry fee = area under individual demand from 5 to 10 = area of the
  right triangle with vertices (0,5),(5,0),(0,10): height 5, width 5
  Area = 5×5/2 = 12.5   →   Entry fee F = $12.50 per customer
```

### Heterogeneous consumers, arbitrage, and disguised two-part tariffs

With heterogeneous demand, designing the *optimal* two-part tariff is complex (beyond this course's scope) — but the notes flag that one workable strategy is to **divide customers into homogeneous groups and run the two-part-tariff analysis separately on each group** (a preview of combining two-part tariffs with third-degree price discrimination, §7 below).

**What limits two-part tariffs — arbitrage.** If a good is easily resold (soda), a two-part tariff collapses: an "arbitrageur" pays the entry fee once, buys in bulk, and resells to others below the two-part price, since his average cost is only $p + F/q$ and falls toward $p$ as $q$ grows. **This is why we see two-part tariffs for hard-to-resell goods/services** (amusement park rides, phone minutes, club memberships, tech-support plans) but not for easily-resold packaged goods.

Firms defeat arbitrage in three main ways, all worth recognizing in the wild:
1. **Packaging.** Selling a good in fixed bundle sizes (e.g., 2-lb sugar bags at $2p+cs$ per bag) makes the entry fee "embedded" per-package rather than paid once and amortized over unlimited units.
2. **Metering / tying.** The entry fee is the price of a *durable good* (a razor handle, a camera, a punch-card machine) and the per-unit charge is the price of a *complementary consumable* the firm can control (blades, film, punch cards) — this only works if the firm can legally/technically prevent third parties from supplying the complement (IP protection for Gillette/Polaroid; historically, contractual tying for IBM punch cards, ruled illegal under antitrust law in the US).
3. Setting the **per-unit price to zero and pure entry-fee pricing** where feasible (unlimited-use club memberships).

**Real-life examples given:** amusement parks; cover charges at bars/clubs; Costco-style membership fees (a membership fee + per-item prices *is* a two-part tariff); land-line phone plans (access fee + per-call charge); tiered cell/calling plans (a **multi-part tariff** — monthly fee, some free minutes, then a per-minute charge beyond that, which is a form of second-degree price discrimination via quantity discounts, §6 below).

## 5. Third-degree price discrimination (§5.5) — group pricing

**Definition:** charging different prices/tariffs to different, *identifiable* groups of consumers (by age, student status, membership, geography, etc.).

### The unconstrained case

**Result 37:** with $N$ distinct populations and no capacity constraint, the firm maximizes profit by choosing $q_i^*$ for each population $i$ such that **all groups' marginal revenues are equal to each other and to marginal cost evaluated at total output**:
```
MR_1(q_1*) = MR_2(q_2*) = ... = MR_N(q_N*) = MC(Σ q_i*)
```
Each group is then charged its own group-specific price $P_i(q_i^*)$ off its own inverse demand curve.

**Example 19 (pharmaceutical, worked in full):** $C(Q)=Q^2/1000$ ⟹ $MC=Q/500$. Two populations: Americans $D_1(p)=100{,}000-1000p$ (for $p\le100$); Canadians $D_2(p)=150{,}000-2000p$ (for $p\le75$).
```
Invert:  P1(q1) = 100 − q1/1000        P2(q2) = 75 − q2/2000
MR1(q1) = 100 − q1/500                 MR2(q2) = 75 − q2/1000

Set MR1 = MR2 = MC(q1+q2):
  100 − q1/500 = (q1+q2)/500
  75  − q2/1000 = (q1+q2)/500

Solving: q1* = 18,750,  q2* = 12,500
Prices:  P1(18,750) = 81.25    P2(12,500) = 68.75
Profit = 18,750×81.25 + 12,500×68.75 − 31,250²/1000 = $1,406,250
```
Compare to the best the firm could do under **nondiscriminatory simple pricing** over the *combined* market: solving that case gives $q^*=31{,}250$ at a single price of \$72.92, with profit **\$1,302,080** — strictly less than third-degree discrimination's \$1,406,250. **Result: third-degree price discrimination can never do worse than simple pricing, and does strictly better whenever the optimal group prices end up unequal** ($P_1(q_1^*)\ne P_2(q_2^*)$).

### The capacity-constrained case

**Result 38:** if the firm faces a binding capacity constraint $K$ across two groups $S$ and $N$ (e.g., a train with fixed seats), the optimal split $(\hat q_S,\hat q_N)$ instead equates the two groups' marginal revenues **to each other directly** (not to marginal cost, since total output is fixed by $K$):
```
MR_S(q_S) = MR_N(q_N)      and      q_S + q_N = K
```
**Example 20 (worked, train tickets):** $P_S(q_S)=401-q_S$ (students), $P_N(q_N)=1001-q_N$ (non-students), $K=500$ seats.
```
Unconstrained optimum: qS*=200, qN*=500 → sums to 700 > 500 (infeasible)

Constrained solution:
  401 − 2qS = 1001 − 2qN   and   qS + qN = 500
  → q̂S = 100,  q̂N = 400
  Prices: PS(100) = $301,   PN(400) = $601
```
**Interpretation:** the opportunity cost of selling a seat to a student is exactly the marginal revenue forgone from *not* selling that seat to a non-student — the standard "shadow price of capacity" logic.

### Arbitrage is the binding constraint on third-degree discrimination

Because different groups face different prices, there's always an incentive for a low-price buyer to resell to a high-price buyer. Third-degree price discrimination is viable only where the firm can **prevent arbitrage**: checking ID (age/student discounts), geography with high transport costs (why firms can charge very different prices in West Virginia vs. California but must charge similarly in adjacent Bay Area counties), or legal/contractual restriction. Pharmaceutical companies charging different prices in different countries is the textbook example — and one that works only as long as parallel importation/resale is restricted or costly.

## 6. Second-degree price discrimination (§5.6) — induced revelation

**Definition:** when the firm **cannot directly observe** which group a buyer belongs to, but can design a menu of offerings that induces each buyer to self-select ("reveal") their type by choosing the option intended for them.

### Quality distortion (versioning)

Classic example: airlines can't directly tell business travelers from tourists, but business travelers value flexibility much more. The airline offers an **unrestricted** ticket (no Saturday-night-stayover requirement) and a **restricted** ticket, pricing them so that:
- **Participation constraints**: both types get non-negative surplus from *some* ticket.
- **Incentive-compatibility (revelation) constraints**: each type prefers the ticket "intended" for them over the other type's ticket.

**Result 39 (general insight):** the type with *valuable private information it wants to conceal* (the business traveler, who doesn't want the airline to know she'd pay more) has a **binding revelation constraint**; the type with nothing to hide (the tourist) has only a binding **participation constraint**.

**Result 40 (information rent):** the type who wants to conceal information earns some surplus — an **information rent** — while the type with nothing to hide earns **zero** surplus (priced exactly at their reservation value). In the airline example: the restricted (tourist) ticket is priced at exactly the tourist's valuation $v_\tau^r$ (zero rent); the unrestricted (business) ticket is priced at $v_b^u - (v_b^r - v_\tau^r)$ — strictly *below* the business traveler's full valuation $v_b^u$, leaving her an information rent equal to $v_b^r - v_\tau^r$.

**Worked numeric example (from the notes):** $v_b^u=500$, $v_b^r=200$, $v_\tau^u=200$, $v_\tau^r=100$ ⟹ optimal prices $p_*^r=100$, $p_*^u=400$. With $N_b=60$, $N_\tau=70$: the two nondiscriminatory single-price options yield \$30,000 (price at $v_b^u=500$, sell to business only) or \$26,000 (price at $v_\tau^u=200$, sell to both) — **price discrimination via versioning yields \$31,000**, beating both.

**Example 21 (software versioning, worked in full) — directly analogous to the Mercedes EV case:** a software firm can sell a full-featured "office" version and a deliberately crippled "home" version, removing $h\times100\%$ of advanced features. Office buyers value the full version at \$500 and the crippled version at $500-100h$; home buyers value the full version at \$350 and the crippled version at $350-25h$. With 100,000 office buyers and $N_H$ home buyers:
```
Home buyers' participation constraint (binding): pH = 350 − 25h
Office buyers' revelation constraint (binding):  pO = pH + 100h = 350 + 75h

Profit = 100,000(350+75h) + N_H(350−25h)

If N_H < 300,000: optimal h = 1  → cripple the home version completely
If N_H > 300,000: optimal h = 0  → don't bother versioning at all
```
**This is the general logic of deliberately "hobbling" a product** (the notes explicitly cite Intel "brain-damaging" chips into cheaper versions, and "home" vs. "office" software editions) — degrading a low-end version isn't wasteful engineering; it is the mechanism that makes the high-end version's premium price *credible* by making the low-end version unattractive to buyers who'd actually pay more.

### Quantity discounts

The other major form of second-degree discrimination: charging less per unit for larger package sizes (why a liter soda bottle costs less than twice a half-liter bottle). With two consumer types — "families" (higher individual demand, $d_f$) and "singles" (lower individual demand, $d_s$) — the *ideal* (but unachievable without direct group identification) third-degree scheme would sell a $q_s$-unit package to singles priced at their full benefit $b(q_s)$, and a $q_f$-unit package to families priced at their full benefit $b(q_f)$.

**The problem:** without direct identification, a family could buy the cheaper $q_s$-unit package instead and earn a positive surplus (area **G** in the notes' figure) rather than the zero surplus the ideal scheme intends. To *induce* the family to buy the larger package, the firm must cut the large-package price enough that the family is just indifferent — this transfers area **G** to the family as an **information rent** (an outcome directly parallel to Result 39/40 above).

**Result (distortion at the bottom):** the firm can do even better than simply granting that rent — by **shrinking the small package below the singles' efficient quantity** ($\hat q_s = q_s - h$), it lowers the family's temptation-package surplus (area **G′** < **G**), letting it raise the family's price and capture the difference (area **H**) as pure profit, at the cost of forgoing some profit (area **J**) from singles who would have bought more at cost. This trade is worthwhile whenever families outnumber (or are comparable in number to) singles. Formally:
```
MR(q̂s) = Ns·ρ(q̂s)                          [marginal revenue from expanding the singles' package]
MC(q̂s) = Ns·c + Nf·(ρf(q̂s) − ρs(q̂s))       [marginal cost: production cost + rising family information rent]
```
At the efficient (undistorted) $q_s$, $MR(q_s) - MC(q_s) = -N_f(\rho_f(q_s)-\rho_s(q_s)) < 0$ — **meaning the profit-maximizing package for singles is smaller than the efficient one.** This is called **distortion at the bottom**: the low-demand type's allocation is deliberately shrunk below the efficient level solely to reduce the information rent paid to the high-demand type. By contrast, there is **no distortion at the top** — the family/high-demand package stays at the efficient quantity $q_f$, since there's no one *above* families whose temptation needs to be managed.

## 7. Combining third- and second-degree tools

The notes explicitly note that third-degree and second-degree price discrimination are not mutually exclusive: a firm can **first split customers into observable groups (third-degree)**, then, within each group, **use two-part tariffs or versioning (second-degree)** to extract further surplus from within-group heterogeneity — e.g., a pharmaceutical company charging different flat per-pill prices to Americans vs. Canadians (third-degree, §5) while separately using packaging/quantity tiers within each country (second-degree quantity discounts, §6) to manage arbitrage and within-country heterogeneity simultaneously.

## Cross-cutting themes

1. **Every pricing scheme in this note is a step toward the "Holy Grail"** (capturing $W(q^{**})$, first-degree/perfect price discrimination) that simple pricing structurally cannot reach. Two-part tariffs get all the way there *only* under homogeneous demand; third- and second-degree discrimination are the practical, partial-capture tools used when consumers are heterogeneous and imperfectly identifiable.
2. **Arbitrage is the universal constraint.** Every scheme above (two-part tariffs, third-degree group pricing, second-degree versioning) works only to the extent the firm can prevent resale between the cheap and expensive options — packaging, metering/tying, IP protection, geography, and ID checks are all, functionally, *anti-arbitrage technologies*.
3. **Information rents are the price of imperfect information.** Whenever the firm cannot directly observe type (second-degree discrimination), the type with something to hide (the higher-valuation type) always retains some surplus — this is not a design flaw, it is a mathematical necessity of any incentive-compatible scheme (Results 39–40), and it's exactly the wedge that "distortion at the bottom" exists to shrink.
4. **Degrading a product on purpose can be profit-maximizing.** Versioning (Example 21) and "distortion at the bottom" (quantity discounts) both formalize the counterintuitive-sounding but very general result that a firm may rationally make its cheap option *worse* than it needs to be, purely to protect the pricing of its premium option.
