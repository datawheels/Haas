## 1. WSJ — "Bowling for \$418? Surge Pricing Creeps Into Restaurants, Movies, Gym Class"

**The setup.** Bowlero charges $418.90 for a holiday-week bowling reservation versus \$208.77 for the identical slot in an off-peak month — more than double for the "same" two lanes/two hours. The article documents the same dynamic-pricing logic spreading to movie theaters (AMC's seat-location pricing), golf courses, cruise excursions, ClassPass gym bookings, and even a fast-casual restaurant chain (Rachel's Kitchen).

**Discussion question 1 — Why might charging different prices at different times be a successful pricing strategy?**

This is **third-degree price discrimination where the segmenting variable is time/date rather than an observable buyer characteristic** (Theory Notes §5). A bowling alley has essentially fixed capacity (a fixed number of lanes) and near-zero marginal cost per additional game once the lane is staffed — exactly the **capacity-constrained third-degree discrimination** case (Result 38). Demand for a Thursday-afternoon slot during winter break (families with no school, no work) is far more inelastic than demand for the identical slot on a random February weekday — holiday-week bowling is a "captive" activity with fewer easy substitutes (other family activities are also crowded/expensive/closed) and less flexibility (families cannot easily reschedule around a specific vacation window). Off-peak weekday demand, by contrast, is highly elastic: a bored weekday afternoon has abundant substitute activities. Applying the capacity-constrained logic of Result 38 (equate marginal revenue across time-segments, subject to the seating/lane constraint), the alley optimally charges the high-elasticity slot a price close to marginal cost and the low-elasticity slot a much higher price — precisely the \$208.77-vs.-\$418.90 pattern observed. This is a "successful" strategy in the narrow economic sense of Result 37: **third-degree discrimination can never do worse, and typically does strictly better, than a single flat price** applied across all times, because it captures surplus from inelastic-demand periods that a flat price would either overcharge (in low-demand periods, losing volume) or undercharge (in high-demand periods, leaving money on the table) relative to.

**Discussion question 2 — The Bowlero CFO states that day/time-based pricing helps "simultaneously maximiz[e] guest happiness through reasonable wait times and maximiz[e] profitability by scaling price when the willingness to pay is there." Do some consumers "maximize their happiness" when they pay higher prices?**

There is a genuine, defensible efficiency argument buried in the CFO's statement, alongside an obvious profit-maximization motive, and the theory notes let us separate the two:
- **The efficiency argument (real, but partial):** without dynamic pricing, a single low price for a fixed-capacity resource at peak times creates *excess demand* — the market doesn't clear, and the good gets rationed by queuing/wait times/no availability instead of by price. Some high-willingness-to-pay customers who would happily pay \$418.90 to guarantee a lane during winter break might otherwise show up and find no lanes available at all, or wait an hour, which is its own (non-monetary) cost. For a subset of customers whose value of *guaranteed availability* exceeds \$418.90, paying the higher price and getting the slot genuinely can leave them better off than the counterfactual of a rationed, uncertain, or unavailable alternative. In this narrow sense, yes — a consumer who was going to be shut out entirely under a static low price can be made better off by a high price that guarantees access.
- **But this is not the same as consumers "maximizing happiness" from paying more in general.** The theory notes' welfare framework (Theory Notes §3) shows that *total* welfare $W(q)$ is maximized when price equals marginal cost — surge pricing above marginal cost during peak times is, from a pure efficiency standpoint, primarily a **transfer of surplus from consumers to the firm**, not a pure efficiency gain, except for the specific slice of consumers who would otherwise have been rationed out entirely. Most of the customers quoted in the article (Alex Yenni, Reed Kremer, Melissa Harans Levenson, Patrick Jandorf) plainly do **not** feel happier paying more — they describe it as "outrageous," a "turnoff," "opaque," and something that "give[s] people a sense of unfairness" (per Dartmouth's Prof. Shumsky, quoted in the article). This matches the theory: **the CFO's statement conflates the marginal, capacity-constrained buyer who benefits from guaranteed access with the average buyer, who is simply having more of their consumer surplus extracted.** The theory notes' distinction between total welfare $W(q)$ (efficiency) and its *split* between consumer surplus and profit is exactly the tension the article surfaces: dynamic pricing can be efficiency-improving (better allocation, less wasteful rationing) while *simultaneously* making most individual consumers worse off in surplus terms — both things are true at once, and the CFO's quote elides the second half.

**Connection to theory:** This article is the cleanest real-world instance of **third-degree price discrimination with capacity constraints (Result 38 / Example 20)** — the exact math of the train-ticket example (students vs. non-students, fixed seats $K$) applies directly to lanes/tee-times/theater seats sold across different demand-elasticity time windows.

## 2. WSJ — "On Orbitz, Mac Users Steered to Pricier Hotels"

**The setup.** Orbitz's internal data showed Mac users spend, on average, meaningfully more per night on hotels than PC users when booking travel. Orbitz used this to steer Mac users toward higher-priced hotel options in search results/rankings (not necessarily an *identical* hotel at a different price, but a systematically pricier *choice set*).

**Discussion question 1 — Do you think Orbitz has information to estimate the price elasticity of demand for hotels by Mac vs. PC users? If so, can the company use this information to target price promotions to only PC users?**

Yes. Orbitz has exactly the kind of data needed: booking histories segmented by device/OS, which reveal average spend, click-through and conversion rates at different price points, and abandonment/comparison-shopping behavior by segment. From this, Orbitz can estimate (even if only approximately) $\varepsilon_D$ separately for the Mac-user population and the PC-user population (Theory Notes §5, the two-population third-degree discrimination setup with $D_1(p)$ for Mac and $D_2(p)$ for PC). If Mac users are on average less price-sensitive (lower $|\varepsilon_D|$ — consistent with Mac users skewing toward a higher-income demographic that also chooses a premium computer brand), the Lerner-rule logic from Class 4 combined with Result 37 here directly implies Orbitz should show Mac users a **higher optimal price** ($-1/\varepsilon_D$ is larger for less-elastic demand) — which is exactly the behavior reported. And yes, this information supports **targeted promotions to PC users specifically**: if PC-user demand is more elastic, a promotional discount aimed only at that segment is a demand-elasticity-targeted price cut of exactly the kind Result 37 recommends — cutting price more where it will recruit more incremental volume, while leaving the less price-sensitive segment's price (and margin) untouched. This is third-degree price discrimination via device/OS as the *identifying, hard-to-arbitrage characteristic* (a consumer can't easily "resell" a hotel booking made on someone else's operating system, so the arbitrage problem that limits other forms of price discrimination, Theory Notes §5, is largely absent here).

**Discussion question 2 — Do you think Orbitz's price discrimination strategy may be helping or harming consumers?**

Both, depending on which consumer and which comparison point:
- **Harming (relative to what an individual Mac user might get on an OS-blind site):** an individual Mac user searching for the exact same hotel/dates would, all else equal, be shown pricier options or a higher price than an otherwise-identical PC user — a direct transfer of surplus from Mac users to Orbitz, exactly analogous to how, in Result 40, the type with less need to "hide" pays a price closer to their reservation value while the more price-sensitive type is protected.
- **Helping (relative to a single flat price for everyone):** by Result 37, third-degree discrimination can never make the firm worse off than simple pricing, and typically means the *more* price-sensitive group (PC users here) gets a **lower** price than they would under a single non-discriminatory price averaged across both groups. So while Mac users are worse off than under a hypothetical uniform low price, PC users may well be *better* off than under a hypothetical uniform price that averaged in Mac users' higher willingness to pay. Whether discrimination is net "harmful to consumers" as a whole is genuinely ambiguous without knowing the counterfactual uniform price and the relative population sizes — this ambiguity is inherent to price discrimination generally (it always redistributes surplus between segments; whether *total* consumer surplus rises or falls compared to the best feasible uniform price depends on the specifics of the two demand curves).
- **A separate harm not captured by the pure elasticity framework:** unlike the article's other examples (a train ticket priced differently for students vs. non-students, where the segmentation is *transparent*), OS-based steering is **opaque and non-consensual** — Mac users are not told they are seeing a systematically pricier result set, which raises a fairness/informed-consent concern distinct from the pure welfare-economics question. The theory notes' framework (which types of price discrimination are "legitimate") is silent on this distinction; it evaluates only efficiency and surplus transfer, not disclosure.

**Connection to theory:** A direct real-world case of **third-degree price discrimination (Theory Notes §5)** using an *inferred* rather than directly stated characteristic (device/OS as a low-cost, hard-to-arbitrage proxy for underlying elasticity) — contrast with the Class 4 chocolate-company article, where the firm reprices in response to its *own* cost shocks rather than segmenting customers.

## 3. NYT — "Costco Is Cracking Down on Membership Sharing"

**The setup.** Costco's entire business model is a textbook **two-part tariff**: a membership fee (entry fee $F$) plus item prices set close to cost (per-unit charge $p \approx MC$, or even a thin markup). Self-checkout lanes made it much easier for a non-member to enter the store using a shared/borrowed membership card, threatening the revenue that the entry-fee side of the model depends on.

**Discussion question 1 — What problem did self-checkout lanes introduce that interfered with Costco's pricing strategy?**

Self-checkout is, in the language of Theory Notes §4, a **new arbitrage channel** for Costco's two-part tariff. Costco's whole pricing logic (Result 36) depends on capturing consumer surplus almost entirely through the entry fee, while pricing individual items close to marginal cost — precisely because that combination gets closest to the "Holy Grail" (perfect price discrimination) among a roughly homogeneous shopper base. That structure only works if **everyone who buys goods at Costco's near-cost prices has also paid the entry fee**. Traditionally, a cashier checking a membership card at a staffed register enforced this — a member had to physically present their card and (often) their photo ID at each transaction, and a companion shopping alone without a card would typically be stopped. **Self-checkout removed that consistent human enforcement point**: a shopper could scan a borrowed or shared membership card's barcode without the same friction/scrutiny a cashier interaction provides, allowing non-members to buy Costco's near-marginal-cost goods *without* ever paying the entry fee that makes those low prices profitable in the first place. In effect, self-checkout **reopened the arbitrage/free-rider problem** that the entry-fee structure is specifically designed to close (Theory Notes §4's discussion of why two-part tariffs require restricting access to non-payers, just like an amusement park needs a gate).

**Discussion question 2 — How would you evaluate the costs and benefits of checking IDs at self-checkout versus eliminating self-checkout altogether?**

Framing this as a straightforward cost-benefit comparison, using the two-part-tariff logic directly:

*Checking IDs at self-checkout:*
- **Benefit:** restores the enforcement mechanism that protects entry-fee revenue (the core of Costco's profit under Result 36 — recall that in the homogeneous-consumer two-part tariff, essentially *all* of profit comes from $F$, with the per-unit price merely covering marginal cost) while preserving self-checkout's separate cost-saving benefit (lower labor cost per transaction, faster throughput, which is itself a form of marginal-cost reduction that indirectly supports a lower per-unit price / lower $MC$).
- **Cost:** added friction and slower self-checkout transactions (undermining some of self-checkout's speed advantage), the labor/technology cost of ID verification (a scanner or an attendant spot-checking IDs), and a real risk of alienating loyal members who resent being "carded" repeatedly (a customer-experience cost that isn't in the formal two-part-tariff model but matters for retention and thus for $N$, the number of paying members in Result 36's profit formula).

*Eliminating self-checkout entirely:*
- **Benefit:** returns to the fully reliable enforcement of staffed checkout, closing the arbitrage channel completely with certainty.
- **Cost:** gives up *all* of self-checkout's throughput/labor-cost benefits, not just the arbitrage-related downside — a much larger cost than the targeted ID-check option, since it discards an efficiency-improving technology to solve what is really a narrower enforcement problem.

**The economically sound conclusion (and the one Costco is reportedly pursuing):** ID checks at self-checkout are the more efficient fix because they target the *specific* margin causing revenue leakage (unpaid entry fees) without sacrificing the *separate* efficiency gain self-checkout provides (lower transaction cost). Eliminating self-checkout entirely would be over-correcting — solving an arbitrage problem by discarding an unrelated efficiency improvement, when a much narrower, cheaper intervention (spot ID checks) addresses the actual leak.

**Connection to theory:** Directly illustrates **Theory Notes §4** — real-life two-part tariffs (Costco explicitly named as the running example in the lecture notes themselves) and the arbitrage constraint that limits them. It also connects to Result 36's insight that under homogeneous demand, a two-part tariff can approach the Holy Grail — but *only if* the entry fee is actually collected from every buyer, which is precisely the enforcement problem self-checkout created.

## 4. CNN — "You Can Make Your Mercedes EV Go Faster"

**The setup.** Mercedes sells some of its EVs with a software-locked acceleration boost: the same physical hardware is capable of faster 0-60 times, but the full performance is only unlocked if the owner pays an additional subscription/one-time fee to remove the software limiter.

**Discussion prompt — its relationship to versioning and price discrimination.**

This is close to a *perfect* real-world instance of the **software-versioning example worked in full in the Theory Notes (§6, Example 21)**. The parallel is almost exact:
- In Example 21, a software firm produces a single underlying product but deliberately removes $h\times100\%$ of features to create a cheaper "home" version alongside the full "office" version — the *marginal cost* of producing either version is essentially identical (same underlying software), so the entire price difference is pure versioning-driven price discrimination, not a cost-based difference.
- In the Mercedes case, the underlying **hardware is identical** across the "locked" and "unlocked" cars — Mercedes is not manufacturing a physically weaker motor for the base trim and a physically stronger one for the performance trim. The acceleration limiter is a **software crippling of a fundamentally single, more-capable product**, exactly matching the theory notes' description of Intel "brain-damaging" chips or software firms selling artificially hobbled "home" editions.

**Why this is second-degree, not third-degree, price discrimination:** Mercedes cannot directly observe which buyers would pay more for faster acceleration and which wouldn't (unlike, say, a student discount, where the firm directly verifies group membership). Instead, Mercedes offers a **menu** — buy the base car, or buy the base car plus the acceleration unlock — and lets customers **self-select** by revealing their own willingness to pay through their purchase choice. This is precisely the induced-revelation mechanism at the heart of second-degree price discrimination (Theory Notes §6): performance-sensitive buyers (who value speed highly) reveal their type by paying for the unlock; more budget-conscious or less performance-focused buyers reveal their type by not paying, and get the artificially limited (but perfectly functional for ordinary driving) version.

**Applying Results 39–40 (information rent):** the buyer who cares intensely about acceleration and would pay a lot for it is the type with "something to hide" from Mercedes' pricing team (their true willingness to pay) — under an optimal scheme, this buyer still earns some information rent (the unlock price is set below their absolute maximum willingness to pay, or else they'd be indifferent to buying at all, per the participation constraint), while the buyer with low willingness to pay is priced right at their reservation value on the base package (zero rent), exactly mirroring the office/home software structure in Example 21.

**Why deliberately hobble the hardware instead of building two different motors?** The theory notes' framing makes this a purely economic (not engineering) decision: manufacturing one hardware platform and cheaply differentiating it in software is almost certainly lower marginal cost than manufacturing two genuinely different drivetrains, while still allowing Mercedes to price-discriminate between performance-sensitive and price-sensitive buyers. The "distortion at the bottom" logic (Theory Notes §6, quantity-discounts section) generalizes cleanly here too: Mercedes doesn't need the base (locked) version to be *maximally* crippled from an engineering-cost standpoint — it needs it crippled *just enough* that the performance-sensitive buyer prefers paying for the unlock over keeping the base car, which is exactly the incentive-compatibility logic, not a cost-minimization one.

**Connection to theory:** The single cleanest, most literal application in either Class 4 or Class 5 of **Example 21's versioning framework** — same core product, artificially differentiated via software rather than genuinely different manufacturing costs, priced to induce self-selection by willingness-to-pay for performance.

## Cross-cutting themes

1. **All four articles are instances of the same underlying move: going beyond simple pricing to capture more of $W(q^{**})$**, using the specific tool that fits the firm's information and enforcement constraints — third-degree discrimination by time (Bowlero) or by inferred characteristic (Orbitz/Mac), a two-part tariff (Costco), and second-degree discrimination via versioning (Mercedes).
2. **Every scheme's viability hinges on defeating arbitrage**, and each article shows a different anti-arbitrage technology in action: Bowlero/Orbitz rely on the segmenting variable (time, device) being inherently hard to arbitrage across; Costco relies on ID enforcement at the point of sale; Mercedes relies on the unlock being a *software* lock tied to the vehicle's identity, which (unlike a punch-card or razor blade) cannot be physically resold or shared the way a membership card can.
3. **Price discrimination is always, mechanically, a transfer of surplus alongside (sometimes) an efficiency gain** — the Bowlero and Orbitz discussion questions both turn on separating these two effects. A pricing scheme can improve allocative efficiency (better matching scarce capacity to the highest-value uses, as with surge pricing) while simultaneously making a majority of individual consumers worse off in surplus terms than they would be under a hypothetical (but often infeasible) uniform low price — both are true at once, and conflating them is the analytical mistake to avoid in every one of these case discussions.
4. **Information rents (Results 39–40) show up whenever a firm cannot directly identify buyer type** — Mercedes' unlock buyers and Orbitz's inferred-elasticity Mac users are both, structurally, "types who reveal information through their choices," and the pricing menu in each case is built around exactly the participation/incentive-compatibility logic from the Theory Notes' airline-ticket and software-versioning examples.
